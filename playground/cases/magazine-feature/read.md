FEATURE | OCEANS

The Kelp Gardeners

Off the coast of Carrow Point, a crew of volunteer divers is replanting an
underwater forest one rope at a time.

By Ines Calloway | Photographs by Ruben Okoro

T he first thing you notice at nine

metres is the silence. The second is
the urchins: thousands of them,
purple and patient, grazing a seabed that
ten years ago was a forest so dense that
divers needed a compass to find their way
out of it. Today the rock is bare enough to
read the ripple marks in the sand.
Maren Tolliver has been diving this reef
since she was fifteen. She remembers the
canopy closing over her head at slack tide,
the light going green and dim, the cod
hanging motionless between the stalks.
When the forest began to thin in a run of
warm summers, she assumed it would
recover the way it always had. It did not.
What followed was a slow collapse that
scientists call a phase shift. Warm water
weakened the kelp; the urchins that the
kelp had kept in check by starving them of

light and space moved in; and once the
urchins held the rock, young kelp could no
longer take hold. A barren, it turns out, is
stable in its own way.
The Carrow Point project began with
six divers and a borrowed boat. Each

weekend they cleared urchins from a plot
the size of a tennis court, then lowered
ropes seeded in a shore tank with kelp
spores. The first year, almost nothing grew.
The second year, the ropes came up furred
with brown blades as long as a forearm.
Now the group has more than two
hundred volunteers, a permit from the
county, and a waiting list. Their method

“We are not saving the forest. We are giving it a
reason to come back on its own.”

has not changed much. Divers work in
pairs, one clearing and one counting, and
every rope is logged with its depth, its
seed batch and the date it went into the

water.
The counting
matters more than the
clearing, says Tolliver.
Without it, nobody
could tell a good year
from a lucky one, and
the funders who now
pay for the boat fuel
would have moved on
long ago.
The logs have
started to tell a story.
Plots cleared in the

first two seasons now hold kelp taller than
a diver, and the cod have returned to the
deeper edges. Plots cleared later, after a
summer of record water temperatures, are
patchier, and some ropes have been
stripped bare again by urchins drifting in

from the unmanaged reef next door.
That edge is the project’s biggest worry.
Clearing a plot buys time, but the barren
beyond it keeps sending urchins in, and the
volunteers cannot clear the whole coast.
Tolliver wants the county to fund a
commercial urchin harvest on the outer
reef, an idea that has divided the harbour.
The lobster crews who fish that reef are

wary of anything that brings more boats
out.
