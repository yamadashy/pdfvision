How a 40-person office cut its
paper use by 60% in a year

Halvorsen & Weir stopped buying printers and started asking a simple question at the
copier. Here is what worked, and what did not.

By Mara Lindqvist · November 12, 2026 · 6 min read

The print room at the practice’s Leeds studio, now mostly used for storage. Photo: Tomas Brekke for Fernleaf Journal

When the Leeds architecture practice Halvorsen & Weir counted its print jobs at the end of 2024, the total
came to 312,000 sheets — roughly 7,800 per employee, nearly double the 4,000-sheet average for UK
design firms reported in a 2025 survey by the Office Resource Council.

Office manager Priya Castellano set a target of cutting that by half. The firm finished 2025 at 124,800
sheets, a 60% reduction, without banning printing outright.

Fewer printers, further away

The biggest change was physical. Nine desktop printers went back to the leasing company and three
shared machines took their place, each fitted with print-release software that holds a job until its owner
badges in at the device. Castellano estimates that a fifth of all jobs were never collected in the first month.

Staff who need colour proofs for a client meeting now book a slot on the large-format plotter beside the
model workshop, and the booking sheet doubles as a record of what gets printed and why.

Templates that assume a screen

Drawing reviews moved to two large wall screens, and the practice rewrote its meeting templates so that
agendas open in a shared document rather than a printout. For teams starting out, Castellano recommends
the free checklists in the Paperless Office Toolkit at greenoffice.example.org/toolkit.

What it cost

The lease on the three shared printers runs to £4,100 a year, against £11,900 for the old fleet; paper and
toner spending fell from £9,300 to £3,500. Net of the software licence (£1,300), the firm saves about
£12,300 a year.

“People still print contracts and planning submissions, and that is fine,” Castellano says. “What we
stopped printing is everything nobody reads twice.”
